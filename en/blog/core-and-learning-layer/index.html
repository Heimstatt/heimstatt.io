We called it our key contribution. It wasn't new.

Our framework's README has a table that lists where each of our ideas comes from. One row is about separating a skill's stable core from its learning layer. It credited two sources for the ingredients and called putting them together our "Key contribution".

For this series we set ourselves a rule: before we repeat a claim in public, we look for evidence against it. This is the post where the rule cost us something.

The idea

Every procedure our team uses is a folder with three files. SKILL.md says what to do. living-checklist.md collects what we learned by doing it. revisionslog.md records changes to the first file.

The point of the split is that the two kinds of text age differently. The method changes rarely and should change on purpose. Experience arrives every time somebody uses the method, and most of it is small: a trap, an exception, a check that turned out to matter. If both live in one file, either the method drowns in anecdotes or the anecdotes never get written down. Our framework says the learning layer is "never merged back" into the core.

What we found when we looked

It took three searches to find the same separation elsewhere.

The documentation of Claude Code, one of the tools we work in, distinguishes two kinds of memory. One is the instruction file: "instructions you write". The other is automatic memory: "notes Claude writes itself based on your corrections and preferences". The comparison table in that documentation even has a row for it. Who writes it: you, or Claude.

A research paper from October 2025, on what its authors call agentic context engineering, describes contexts as "evolving playbooks that accumulate, refine, and organize strategies", updated in small structured additions instead of being rewritten. That is kin to our checklist in one respect: knowledge is added in small pieces instead of rewriting the whole. It differs in two others. The playbook is also pruned and de-duplicated, and there is no separate core that stays put.

Anthropic's own article on skills recommends letting the model record its successes and mistakes in the skill. Karpathy's autoresearch splits a repository by author: the human writes the instructions, the agent writes the code.

None of these is identical to what we do. But the insight that instructions and experience should be kept apart, and that the model can be the one writing the experience, is plainly not ours alone. We have changed the row in our table. It now reads: our arrangement, not a new idea.

What is actually ours

Smaller things, and more concrete ones.

We keep experience per procedure, not per project and not per machine. The lessons from security reviews sit next to the security review method and travel with it into every project that uses it.

We keep it in a shared Git repository. Tool-specific memory belongs to one tool on one computer. Ours is a file that different agents on different models from different labs can read and write, with every change attributed. In our case that is three agents on models from two labs. Three is a deliberate number: these files belong to our first layer, the agents that lead the work together with the human in our team. We did not extend the arrangement to every worker agent underneath.

And we log changes to the core separately, so that a quiet edit to the method does not look like one more lesson learned.

What our own numbers say

We counted commits across the 17 skills in our working repository, as of 10 October 2026. One commit that only renamed the core files is left out.

FileCommits
SKILL.md, the core66
living-checklist.md, the learning layer328
revisionslog.md, the change log36

The split does what it is meant to do. The learning layer changes about five times as often as the core. For our token-budget skill the ratio is 97 to 3, for security reviews 56 to 3.

The same count shows where we do not follow our own design. In five skills the core has more commits than the learning layer, among them three that each describe a single service. There, experience evidently went straight into the method. And three of our service skills have a change log with no commits at all, against four commits to the core in each. The rule says every change to the core is logged. In those three folders nobody logged anything.

We also cannot answer a question a careful reader will ask: when does a lesson become part of the method, and who decides? Our framework's answer is that it never does. In practice the cores did change 66 times, and we have not checked how many of those changes began as a lesson.

What we would keep

Keep instructions and experience in different files. Enough people have arrived at that independently that we would now call it a finding rather than a preference.

Decide where the experience lives. We chose the procedure it belongs to and a place everyone can reach.

And check your own table of sources before someone else does. Ours claimed one idea too many.
